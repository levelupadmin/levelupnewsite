Most articles about filmmaking courses in India are lists of institutes with the fees copied off a brochure. That is not useful when you are deciding what to do with a year of your life and a large amount of money. So this compares the real options on the two things that actually decide it: what it costs you in money, and what it costs you in time you cannot spend earning.

We run one of the programmes on this list. It is included on the same terms as everything else, including where it is the wrong choice.

## The comparison

| Programme | Fee | Duration | Eligibility | Seats | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FTII Pune**, PG Certificate | ₹1.58 L | 1 year | Graduation | ~40 across specialisations | The cheapest route to a recognised qualification |
| **FTII Pune**, PG Diploma | ₹2.99 L | 2 years | Graduation | ~40 across specialisations | Direction and screenwriting, or cinematography, taught deeply |
| **Whistling Woods**, BFA Filmmaking | ₹28.5 L | 3 years | 10+2 | 200 | School leavers who want the Mumbai industry network |
| **Whistling Woods**, MFA Filmmaking | ₹22 L – ₹27.5 L | 2 years | Graduation | 15 per specialisation | Specialising after a first degree |
| **Forge Filmmaking** (LevelUp) | ₹85 K – ₹1.05 L | 15 days, online then in person | None | About 24 | Working professionals who cannot stop earning |
| **Regional film schools** | Varies widely | 1–3 years | Varies | Varies | Lower cost, entry into a regional industry |

Fees for FTII and Whistling Woods are as listed on Collegedunia and CollegeDekho respectively, checked 25 August 2026. Verify against the current prospectus before you apply, because institutes revise fees between admission cycles.

## FTII Pune

The best value on this list by a wide margin, and the hardest to get into.

A two-year PG Diploma in Direction and Screenwriting or Cinematography costs about ₹2.99 lakh in total. A one-year PG Certificate in Video Editing or Television Direction is about ₹1.58 lakh. Both require a completed degree. Roughly 40 places are available across all specialisations, for the whole country.

**Pick FTII if** you can commit one to three years full-time, you can sit a competitive entrance process, and a government-recognised qualification matters to you.

**Do not pick FTII if** you need to keep earning. There is no part-time route, and the entrance process assumes you can reorganise your life around the result.

## Whistling Woods International

The most expensive option here and the most industry-connected. A three-year BFA in Filmmaking runs to about ₹28.5 lakh, with 200 seats and dual electives across directing, cinematography, editing and producing. The two-year MFA is ₹22 lakh to ₹27.5 lakh with 15 seats per specialisation. Admission is by their own entrance test plus portfolio and interview.

**Pick Whistling Woods if** the Mumbai film industry is where you intend to work, you want to be inside that network from day one, and the fee is genuinely affordable to your family.

**Do not pick Whistling Woods if** the fee would require debt you cannot service on an assistant's salary. Entry-level film work in India does not pay quickly, and a ₹28 lakh loan against that is a hard start.

## Short residential intensives

A newer category, and the one we are in. Forge Filmmaking costs roughly ₹85,000 to ₹1,05,000 depending on the edition and season. It runs for 15 days, starting online and finishing in person, takes about 24 people per edition, and has no eligibility requirement. Its whole design assumption is that you have a job you cannot leave.

**Pick a short intensive if** you are working, you want to find out whether you can actually do this before restructuring your life around it, and you learn better by making something than by studying it for two years.

**Do not pick a short intensive if** you want a recognised degree, or you are hoping the certificate itself will get you hired. It will not. What you leave with is work you made and people who saw you make it.

## Regional film schools

Often overlooked and frequently the right answer. SRFTI in Kolkata, the state film institutes and several private schools in Chennai, Hyderabad and Kochi run one to three-year programmes at a fraction of the Mumbai cost, with direct routes into their regional industries. If you intend to work in Malayalam, Tamil, Telugu or Bengali cinema, a regional school is often a better network than a national one.

Fees and formats vary too widely to summarise honestly here. Check each institute directly.

## So which one

Answer one question first: **can you stop earning for a year or more?**

If yes, and you can clear a competitive entrance, FTII gives you the most training per rupee of anything in the country. If yes and the money is not a constraint, Whistling Woods buys you the deepest industry network. If no, a short residential intensive is the only category that fits your life, and the honest trade is that you get less depth than a two-year course, because two years is longer than a week.

If you are unsure whether you want this at all, do not spend ₹28 lakh finding out. Make something small first.

## Common questions

**Do I need a film degree to work in Indian film?**
No. A great many working directors, editors and cinematographers in India have no formal film qualification. What gets you hired is work people can watch and someone willing to vouch for you. A course is one efficient way to produce both, not the only one.

**Is FTII worth the entrance difficulty?**
On cost per hour of training, nothing else comes close. The constraint is time and competition, not money.

**Can I learn filmmaking online instead?**
You can learn the theory online for very little. What you cannot get online is a crew, a deadline, and someone experienced watching you make decisions under pressure. That is what any of the in-person options are actually selling.

**What does a short intensive actually get me?**
A piece of finished work, a set of people who have seen you work, and a realistic sense of whether you want this. Not a qualification.
